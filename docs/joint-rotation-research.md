# Joint & Tendon Rotation — Research and Proposed Pool

Research backing a daily rotating pool of joint/tendon work covering the six
regions that account for climbing's injury load: **shoulders, elbows, wrists,
hips, knees, ankles**.

Design goal, in the owner's words: *least amount of work for the most amount of
gain, low injury risk.*

Every dose below carries its source. Nothing here is invented; where a number is
standard practice rather than a cited protocol, it says so.

---

## 1. Why this is worth ~10 minutes a day

**Strength training cuts sports injuries by roughly two thirds, and the effect is
dose-dependent.** Lauersen et al.'s meta-analysis (7,738 participants) found a
~68% reduction in injury rate across four studies, with acute *and* overuse
injuries both reduced. A 10% increase in strength-training volume bought a
further >4 percentage-point drop in risk.
([Lauersen et al., BJSM 2018](https://research.regionh.dk/en/publications/strength-training-as-superior-dose-dependent-and-safe-prevention-of-))

Two consequences for this design:

1. Consistency beats intensity. The first few sets per week are the ones that
   buy the reduction; there is no need to train prehab hard.
2. A dose that actually happens every day beats a better dose that happens
   monthly. This is the entire argument for rotation over a fixed checklist.

**Ceiling on session length.** Tendon goes refractory to loading after ~10
minutes; longer sessions add wear without adaptation. Two short sessions 6–8h
apart roughly double the collagen-synthesis response versus one long one.
([Baar, summarised](https://bracelab.com/clinicians-classroom/reconsidering-isometrics-insights-from-the-work-of-dr-keith-baar))

So ~10 minutes is not a compromise — it is the evidence-backed ceiling for a
single tendon-loading session.

## 2. Where climbers actually get hurt

| Region | Share | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Upper limb overall | ~70% of all climbing injuries | [AMSSM](https://sportsmedref.amssm.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Rock-Climbing-Injuries-Basics-JL.pdf) |
| Hand / wrist | 60% of upper-limb injuries | [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0363018815001061) |
| Shoulder / elbow | 40% of upper-limb injuries | same |
| Finger pulleys (A2/A4) | up to 33% of *all* climbing injuries | same |
| Lower limb | ~20%, ankle leading | AMSSM |

Overuse outnumbers acute in adults (Lutter: 68% / 32%), and the 2025 systematic
review names **fingers and shoulders** as the two primary overuse sites.
([Systematic review, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12821603/))

Fingers are already covered by the existing plan (Abrahangs + PIMA + max hangs).
Everything else in that table is currently uncovered or barely covered.

## 3. The four loading mechanisms

These have incompatible frequency requirements, which is why one global rotation
rule cannot serve all of them.

| Mechanism | Dose | Frequency | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Collagen synthesis** | ~40% MVC, 5–10 min | 2×/day, 6–8h apart | Baar |
| **Magnitude / stiffness** | heavy, slow (3s/3s), full range | ~3×/week | [HSR vs eccentric meta-analysis](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844024152024) |
| **Analgesic isometric** | 70% MVC, 30–45s × 3–5 | as needed | [Standard tendinopathy prescription](https://www.physio-network.com/blog/improve-isometric-exercise-prescription/) |
| **Antagonist rebalance** | 80% MES, 5s × 10, 4 sets | 2×/week | [Finger flexion:extension study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10701375/) |

Isolated eccentrics are **not** superior to heavy slow resistance and may leave
worse eccentric strength deficits — so nothing below is eccentric-only for its
own sake.

The 70% MVC / 30–45s isometric is the cheapest useful unit in the whole table:
~2–4 minutes per joint, and it is the format most of the pool below uses.

## 4. The pool

Six targets. Each rotates stalest-first within its group, on its own interval.
~2–3 slots surface per day, ~10 minutes total.

Legend: **[have]** already in the catalog · **[new]** needs adding

### Shoulder — 2–3×/week

Second-most-common overuse site. Serratus anterior and lower trapezius are
"notoriously weak" in climbers; the scapula should upwardly rotate without
elevating, or the supraspinatus gets pinched.
([Climbing Doctor](https://theclimbingdoctor.com/scapular-instability/),
[Mend](https://www.mendcolorado.com/physical-therapy-blog/2026/3/10/the-top-4-exercises-for-bulletproof-shoulder-in-climbers/))

| Movement | Dose | Note |
|---|---|---|
| External rotation (band / light KB) | 2×15/side | **[have]** |
| Internal rotation (band) | 2×15/side | **[new]** — the neglected half of the pair |
| Prone Y raise / lower-trap | 2×10–12 | **[new]** |
| Serratus wall slide or punch | 2×10–12 | **[new]** |
| Scapular retraction isometric | 30–45s × 3 | **[new]** |
| Turkish get-up (light) | 2–3/side | **[have]** |
| Overcoming wall press | 5s × 1–2 | **[have]** (currently GtG) |

Maintenance dose in the climbing-PT literature is 20–30 min 3×/week once
injury-free; the rotation reaches a comparable weekly volume in smaller pieces.

### Elbow — every other day

Highest-yield antagonist region for a finger-flexor-heavy program. Medial and
lateral epicondylitis are both named climbing injuries. All doses from
[Hörst's climber's-elbow protocol](https://trainingforclimbing.com/treating-climbers-elbow-medial-epicondylitis/)
unless noted.

| Movement | Dose | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Pronator (hammer / lever) | 2×15–20, **5s eccentric** | **[new]** |
| Supinator (same lever, reversed) | 2×15–20 | **[new]** — standard practice, not separately cited |
| Reverse wrist curl / extensor work | 2×15 | **[have]** |
| Neutral-grip mid-range isometric | 15–20s × 2–3 | **[new]** — Hörst's "density hang" |
| Finger extension vs rubber band | 15–25 reps light | **[new]** — Hörst is explicit this is *warm-up and rehab grade only*, not strength |

### Wrist — 2–3×/week

Wrist extension makes the finger flexors contract more forcefully — climbers
exploit this by chicken-winging, which chronically loads the extensors. Training
extensors in **both neutral and extended** positions is the stated gap.
([Hörst](https://trainingforclimbing.com/training-the-wrist-stabilizers/))

| Movement | Dose | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Wide pinch with wrist extension** | Strength: 10s × 3/hand, 30s between, 3 min between sets. Endurance: 30s × 3/hand, 1 min rest | **[new]** — Hörst calls this "the single most overlooked and important" |
| Radial / ulnar deviation | 2×15/side | **[new]** — standard practice |
| Wrist extension, extended position | 2×15 | **[new]** |
| Extensor isometric @ 80% MES | 5s × 10, 4 sets, 2×/wk, 4 wks | **[new]** — [+41% extension strength, −28% imbalance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10701375/). This is a *session*, not a slot |

The same study proposes MFS/MES as a measurable ratio (healthy climber mean
**6.27**, and it worsens with experience). That is a §4E-shaped test the battery
does not currently contain.

### Hip — 2×/week

Heel hooks load the knee through tibial external rotation; the prevention advice
is to open the **whole hip** instead so larger muscles take the load.
([Climbing Doctor](https://theclimbingdoctor.com/common-knee-injuries-while-heel-hooking-diagnosis-rehab-prehab-and-more/))

| Movement | Dose | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Copenhagen adduction | 3×6–10/side, short-lever to start | **[new]** — [meta-analysis](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/sms.70119) supports for groin injury |
| Hip external rotation / 90-90 | 2×10/side | **[new]** — standard practice |
| Goblet squat | 3×10 | **[have]** |

### Knee — 2×/week

Heel hooking risks hamstring tendon, popliteus, LCL, PCL and lateral meniscus.
Passive hanging on a heel hook reduces knee stability; active hamstring/calf
engagement increases it.

| Movement | Dose | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Nordic hamstring curl | 2×5, assisted / partial range to start | **[new]** — [halves hamstring injury rate, RR 0.49](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331367089_Including_the_Nordic_hamstring_exercise_in_injury_prevention_programmes_halves_the_rate_of_hamstring_injuries_A_systematic_review_and_meta-analysis_of_8459_athletes). [High vs low volume: no significant difference](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12572617/) in strength or fascicle adaptation — **low volume is the correct choice here** |
| Stability-ball hamstring curl | 2×10 | **[new]** — mimics the heel hook, adds hip/core stabilisation |
| Mid-range knee-flexion isometric | 30–45s × 3 | **[new]** |

### Ankle — 1–2×/week

Ankle leads lower-limb injuries; bouldering falls are the mechanism.
Proprioceptive training cuts ankle sprain incidence: **RR 0.65, NNT 17**.
Effective doses ranged 5–30 min, 1–5×/week — the low end works, and longer
program *duration* matters more than per-session volume.
([Meta-analysis, 3,726 participants](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1440244014000747),
[evidence-based review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5737043/))

| Movement | Dose | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Single-leg balance, eyes closed | 3×30s/side | **[new]** |
| Unstable-surface balance | 3×30s/side | **[new]** |
| Calf raise, straight + bent knee | 2×15 | **[new]** |
| Drop landing | 2×5 | **[new]** — standard practice, not separately cited |

Combining balance work with strengthening gives the most complete result, which
is why calf raises sit in the same group as the balance drills.

## 5. What happens to the GtG seven

They stop being a separate fixed list and become seeded entries in the pool,
keeping their two existing fields:

- **`trigger`** ("any doorway", "every time you make coffee") is what makes them
  free, and stays.
- **`riskClass`** maps directly onto rotation interval. The two `watch`
  movements — full pull-ups and scapular pull-ups — get a *longer* interval,
  which is precisely §8's own "drop these first at any elbow soreness" expressed
  as a schedule instead of a warning.

Both fields already exist on `Exercise`. No new concept.

## 6. Rotation rules

Four tiers, because §3's mechanisms differ:

| Tier | Contents | Rotation |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Collagen** | Abrahangs ×2, finger warm-up | Never — the 6–8h spacing *is* the prescription |
| **2. Daily tendon isometrics** | One slot per major **non-finger** tendon @ ~70% MVC | Slot is fixed and daily; the *movement* filling it rotates |
| **3. Block-fixed max** | Day 1 fingers ×2/wk, Day 3 pull, §4E battery | Only at block boundaries, so retests compare |
| **4. Rotating pool** | Everything in §4 above that is not tier 2 | Stalest-first per target, on the target's interval |

Tiers 2 and 4 both rotate, but differently: tier 2 rotates *within* a fixed daily
slot, tier 4 rotates *which target comes up*. The underlying rule is the same —
`rotation.ts`'s "least-recently-completed wins, seed order breaks ties" — with a
per-target interval added to decide what is *due*.

### Scope: tier 2 is non-finger only

Fingers are **not** in tier 2. They are already well covered and deliberately
left alone: Abrahangs up to 2×/day (tier 1) plus two finger-specific routines per
week (tier 3). Tier 2 exists for the tendons that currently carry *nothing* —
shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles.

This distinction matters, because §7's "one max-intensity finger session per week
is enough" and §8's "never grease-the-groove" are **finger rules**. They exist
precisely because the fingers already carry Abrahangs, two sessions and climbing.
They do not transfer to a rotator cuff that is loaded by nothing. §8's own
allocation rule — *GtG the things you're not maxing, max the things you're not
GtG-ing* — argues for loading these frequently, since they are currently neither.

### Why tier 2 is ~70% and not max

**The structure is well supported.** Hettinger & Müller (1953), the foundational
isometric training study, had subjects perform **daily 6-second isometrics at
~67% MVC** across elbow flexors and extensors, finger flexors, shoulders, hips,
knees, trunk and ankle — one brief contraction per joint, every day, across
essentially the regions tier 2 covers. Loads were adjusted weekly to hold
relative intensity.
([Overview](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/isometric-training/))

*Caveat, stated rather than buried:* the headline +5%/week has **not** replicated
cleanly. Results vary across protocols and muscle groups, and a protocol
validated in one muscle group does not guarantee transfer to another. Treat the
magnitude as optimistic; the structure — daily, brief, one per joint,
submaximal — is the tested part.

**Supporting mechanisms:** isometrics cause little muscle damage and so carry a
low recovery burden; tenocytes go refractory after ~10 min of load, making
frequency the lever rather than volume; sustained 30s holds produce higher Type I
collagen and scleraxis expression than the same volume as 1s pulses.

**Why not true max, daily.** Two independent literatures converge on ~70% as the
intensity a *daily* dose has evidence behind it — Hettinger's ~67% and the
70% MVC tendinopathy standard. Daily true-max across six joints has no comparable
evidence base. Supporting points, both real but secondary:

- Net collagen synthesis/degradation balance is **negative for up to 36 hours**
  after a loading bout — synthesis peaks ~24h, degradation rises earlier
  ([Kjær 2009](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1600-0838.2009.00986.x),
  [Nature Rev Rheumatol](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrrheum.2010.43)). This
  scales with the loading stimulus, so a brief submaximal effort is not a heavy
  session — it argues against daily *max*, not against daily.
- Tyler Nelson's practical test: **fatiguing protocols need 72h recovery, less
  fatiguing ones need 48h**, and short high-intensity efforts with fast recovery
  beat long high-fatigue ones. 70% × 30–45s × 2 stays in the low-fatigue band;
  true max × 5s × 3–5 sets across six joints does not.

**Resolution:** daily slot, ~70% MVC, 30–45s. Max-effort overcoming work
(PIMA, OI bar pulls) stays in tier 3 at 1–2×/week per pattern.

### Tier 2 slots

One per major non-finger tendon, 2 × 30–45s @ ~70% MVC. ~8–10 min for all six.

| Slot | Movements that can fill it |
|---|---|
| Finger / wrist extensors | Extensor isometric; wide-pinch-with-extension hold |
| Elbow (common flexor / extensor origin) | Neutral-grip mid-range hold; pronator isometric |
| Rotator cuff | External-rotation isometric; scapular retraction hold |
| Adductor | Copenhagen isometric hold (short lever) |
| Hamstring / patellar | Mid-range knee-flexion hold; split-squat isometric |
| Achilles | Isometric calf raise hold, straight and bent knee |

Two prescription details that apply to every slot:

- **Hold at the long muscle length.** Isometrics at longer muscle lengths produce
  greater adaptation than equal volume at short lengths
  ([Oranchuk et al. 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/sms.13375)),
  and Baar reports holds with the tendon lengthened improving compliance by up to
  50%. Each entry should name its long-length position rather than saying "hold".
- **Adjust load weekly to hold ~70% relative intensity**, as Hettinger did. That
  is a progression rule the app can implement instead of leaving to feel.

Because a protocol validated in one muscle group does not guarantee transfer to
another, tracking is per-slot rather than one global dose — which the `target`
field already provides.

Movements marked here also appear in §4's pool. That is intentional and not a
duplicate: the same exercise can be loaded as a 30–45s isometric hold (tier 2) or
run for reps (tier 4), and those are different doses of different mechanisms. The
catalog should carry both prescriptions on one entry rather than splitting it,
the way `pima-finger-pull-*` already carries two variants.

Coverage should be a test, not a hope: assert every target has at least one
entry in the pool, in the style of `warmup.test.ts` asserting the Abrahang
rounds sum to 20.

## 7. Injury-risk notes on the pool itself

The point of this program is fewer injuries, so three entries need care:

- **Nordic hamstring** produces severe DOMS if started at full volume and range.
  Start assisted and partial. The volume evidence says low volume is equally
  effective, so there is no cost to starting small.
- **Copenhagen adduction** likewise — begin with the short-lever (knee-on-bench)
  version.
- **Wide pinch with wrist extension** is a genuine strength exercise, not
  prehab; it belongs at the low end of its range until it is familiar.

Everything else is capacity work at prehab intensity and should never be near
failure.

---

## 8. Scope boundary

This pool is **prophylactic**. Cook & Purdam's analgesic isometrics and the full
Hörst elbow protocol are *treatment for an existing symptom* — a different
decision, deliberately excluded here rather than allowed to drift in.

The 70% MVC / 30–45s isometrics that do appear are included for capacity, not
for pain management.
