# Overcoming Isometrics for Climbers — V5/V6 Plateau-Breaker Program

*An 8-week strength block for an advanced climber (V6–V8 range) using a hangboard, a pull-up bar with multiple grip attachments, and a single 35lb kettlebell.*

---

## 1. The Concept, Briefly

Isometric contractions come in two flavors, and climbers use both constantly without realizing it:

- **Yielding isometrics** — you hold a fixed position against a load (e.g., hanging on a crimp, locking off mid-move). The hold "wins" and you resist it. This is what a standard hangboard *hang* trains.
- **Overcoming isometrics** — you apply maximal force *trying* to move an immovable object (e.g., pulling as hard as you can on a jammed hold, or the explosive "catch" moment when your hand first grabs a hold mid-dyno). Nothing moves, but you're actively trying to move it, which recruits high-threshold motor units that submaximal or even max-yielding efforts don't always reach.

For climbing specifically, overcoming isometrics (sometimes labeled **PIMA** — Pulling/Pushing Isometric Muscle Action) map onto the *latching* phase of grabbing a hold — the split-second of maximal pull before your hand settles into a static grip. Yielding holds (**HIMA**) map onto the static hang/lock-off after you've caught the hold. Both matter; a plateau at V5–V6 is very often a *rate-of-force* problem (catching holds hard and fast) as much as a max-strength problem — which is exactly where overcoming isometrics earn their keep.

Two important caveats going in:
1. **Tendons adapt slower than muscle.** You're advanced enough that finger/pulley injury risk is real. Everything below ramps load gradually — don't skip the ramp weeks.
2. Research directly comparing hangboard methods (Gilmore et al., 2024, *Sports Medicine – Open*) found that combining a low-intensity, long-duration protocol with max-intensity work produced *additive* strength gains — better than either alone. This plan uses that logic: one heavy day, one moderate day, per week.

## 2. How This Fits Your Equipment

| Tool | Role |
|---|---|
| Hangboard | Max Hangs (yielding) + PIMA pulls (overcoming) for fingers |
| Pull-up bar + grip attachments | Overcoming isometric pulls, weighted lock-offs, grip-position variety |
| 35lb kettlebell | Added load for weighted hangs/pull-ups, single-arm rows, antagonist work |

**No campus board is fine** — at your level, campus training adds power but also adds real injury risk, and isn't necessary to break a V5–V6 plateau. Overcoming isometrics get you most of that "explosive catch" adaptation with far less shock loading on pulleys.

## 3. Weekly Template (4 climbing/training days)

- **Day 1 — Fingerboard: Overcoming (PIMA) + Max Hangs**
- **Day 2 — Climbing (technique/volume focus)**
- **Day 3 — Pull/Lock-off Overcoming Isometrics + Antagonist & Core**
- **Day 4 — Climbing (power/projecting focus)**
- **Rest 2–3 days** (recovery is when tendons actually adapt — don't compress this)

---

## 4. Day 1: Fingerboard Session

### A. Warm-up (10–15 min)
Easy hangs on jugs, progressively smaller edges, light wrist/finger mobility, 2–3 sub-maximal hangs on your training edge before working sets.

### B. PIMA — Overcoming Isometric Pulls (the "catch" trainer)
Stand on the ground (or a low box) under the hangboard so your arms are bent ~90°. Grab your working edge and pull down as hard as you can *without lifting your feet off the ground* — you're pulling the board into the wall, not pulling yourself up.

- Grip: half-crimp (rotate in open-hand every other session to protect A2 pulleys)
- Effort: 3–5 sec at **100% max effort**
- Sets: 4–6
- Rest: 3 min between sets
- Progress by feel, not by adding weight — this is about neural recruitment and rate of force, not load

**Rep-structured variant (recommended if tendon adaptation is an explicit goal).** The tendon-loading literature converges on roughly *five sets of four contractions at ~90% of isometric max, held 3 seconds each*, as the dose that drives tendon stiffness and modulus. That is very close to what you're already doing, so if you want to bias toward connective-tissue adaptation rather than pure peak-force expression, run PIMA as:
- 5 sets x 4 reps x 3 sec at ~90% effort, ~10 sec between reps, 3 min between sets

Use this variant for weeks 1–4, then the single-max-effort version above for weeks 5–8 when you want peak force. **Note that this — not the greasing-the-groove work in §8 — is your primary tendon-strengthening intervention.** Magnitude drives tendon adaptation; frequency alone does not.

### C. Max Hangs — Yielding (top-end strength)
Weighted or edge-size-adjusted hangs on a 10–14 sec effort.

- Grip: half-crimp, alternate open-hand
- Load: ~85–90% of your max for that edge (should be very hard by rep 3, not failing before then)
- Sets: 5
- Hang: 7–10 sec
- Rest: 3 min between sets

### E. Baseline & Retest Protocol

Do this once in week 1 (fully rested, after a thorough warm-up) and again in week 8. **Identical conditions both times** — same edge, same grip, same time of day, same warm-up — or the comparison is meaningless.

| Test | Method | Record |
|---|---|---|
| Max hang, half-crimp | Find the max added weight you can hold 7 sec on your standard edge. Work up in 3–5 sets with 3 min rest. Stop at the first failed attempt. | Edge size (mm), added weight, bodyweight |
| Max hang, open-hand | Same protocol, open-hand grip | Same |
| Max pull-up load | Heaviest single strict pull-up (kettlebell via dip belt) | Added weight |
| Lock-off hold, 90° | Longest static hold at bodyweight, one attempt per side | Seconds, each side |

Pick **one** standard edge (14–20mm) and never change it mid-block — changing edge size invalidates the comparison more than any training variable.

Don't test PIMA numerically; there's nothing to measure without a force gauge. Note subjectively how hard you can pull and whether it felt like effort or strain.

**Interpreting week 8:** a 10–20% improvement in max hang load is a good block. Flat numbers with better climbing means the gains were technical. Declining numbers mean you accumulated fatigue faster than adaptation — that's the signal to deload, not to push harder.

### F. Weeks 1–8 Progression

| Week | Focus | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Establish baselines, moderate effort (80%) | Let tendons adapt to load before pushing intensity |
| 3–4 | Increase to 90–95% effort | Add small load increments (1–3%) |
| 5–6 | Peak intensity, PIMA at true max effort | This is where rate-of-force gains show up |
| 7 | Deload — half the volume, same intensity | Prevents accumulated tendon strain |
| 8 | Retest max hang load / PIMA feel | Compare to week 1 |

Every 3rd week (3-on/1-off style), take a lighter week regardless of the schedule above if fingers feel beat up. This is non-negotiable at your training age — pulley injuries end seasons, plateaus don't.

## 5. Day 3: Pulling / Lock-off Overcoming Isometrics + Antagonists

### A. Overcoming Isometric Pulls on the Bar (lock-off/pulling power)
Grab the pull-up bar in your working position (start at the top lock-off angle you're weakest at — usually 90° elbow bend for most climbers). Pull upward into the bar as hard as possible without your body actually moving — brace your feet against the ground or a fixed point so you can't lift off.

- 3 angles per session: near-full-extension, 90° lock-off, near top lock-off (rotate through grips: pull-up bar attachments — narrow, wide, neutral)
- Effort: 5 sec max effort
- Sets: 3 per angle (9 total)
- Rest: 2 min

*(If your bar setup lets you truly hang and pull without leaving the ground, great — that's the cleanest overcoming isometric. If your bar is high enough that you always leave the ground, substitute banded/kettlebell-anchored isometric rows, or do the lock-off portion as a yielding isometric instead: pull up to the lock-off angle and hold with the kettlebell added via a dip belt.)*

### B. Weighted Lock-off Holds (yielding, complements the above)
Pull up to your weakest lock-off angle, hold static.
- 3 holds x 8–10 sec, add kettlebell via dip belt as this gets easy (start bodyweight)
- Rest: 2 min

### C. Kettlebell Work — Power & Structural Base
- Single-arm KB rows: 3 x 8/side — pulling strength, scapular control
- KB goblet squats: 3 x 10 — leg drive matters more than people think for hard bouldering
- KB Turkish get-up (light, technique focus): 2–3/side — full-body tension and shoulder stability

### D. Antagonist & Injury-Prevention Work (don't skip this)
Advanced climbers plateau partly because pulling muscles outpace pushing/stabilizing muscles, which caps how hard you can safely train the pullers.
- Push-ups or dips: 3 x 10–15
- Overcoming isometric wall press (push into a wall/doorframe as hard as possible, 5 sec x 3) — shoulder and pushing strength, zero equipment needed
- Band or KB external rotations for the rotator cuff: 2 x 15/side
- Wrist extensor work (reverse curls or wrist roller if you have one): 2 x 15 — protects the elbow from the finger-flexor-heavy work on Day 1

---

## 6. Climbing Days (2 and 4)

These are just as important as the strength days — the isometric work builds capacity, but *using* that capacity on the wall is what converts it into sending V6.

- **Day 2:** Volume/technique — climb below your limit (V3–V5), focus on precision, footwork, and efficient body positioning. This is where you fix the technical leaks that a plateau often hides.
- **Day 4:** Limit bouldering — try hard moves/problems at or above your current max (V6+), short bursts, full rest between attempts (3–5 min). This is where the new finger and pulling strength gets expressed as actual sends.

---

## 7. Safety Notes Specific to You

- **Ramp before you max.** Weeks 1–2 above are there to load your finger pulleys and elbow tendons before asking for 95–100% efforts. Advanced climbers get hurt by skipping this, not by the max efforts themselves.
- **Warm up fingers thoroughly** before any hangboard work — cold pulleys are the #1 cause of finger injuries in exactly your grade range.
- **One max-intensity finger session per week is enough** at this stage; the second session should stay submaximal even as your PIMA work approaches 100% effort (PIMA at 100% effort for 3–5 sec is still lower total tendon strain than a 90% weighted hang).
- **Track everything.** Note edge size, added weight, and how hangs *felt*, so you can spot a downward trend before it becomes an injury.
- If you feel any sharp or pulley-specific pain (as opposed to normal forearm fatigue), stop the session — this is the difference between a plateau and a torn A2 pulley.

## 8. Greasing the Groove — The Low-Intensity Complement

### What it is
Pavel Tsatsouline's method: pick a movement, do short submaximal sets many times through the day, never near failure, long rests between. Typically 40–70% of max, stopping well before fatigue accumulates. The premise is that strength is a skill — and skills improve with frequent *fresh* practice, not infrequent exhausting practice.

### Why it isn't a competing philosophy
Overcoming isometrics and GtG look opposed (max effort vs. never hard) but they're solving different problems and, critically, **they don't compete for the same recovery budget**:

| | Overcoming isometrics | Greasing the groove |
|---|---|---|
| Intensity | 100% | 40–70% |
| Frequency | 1–2x/week per pattern | Daily, many times |
| Fatigue cost | High neural, low tissue damage | Near zero |
| Trains | Peak force, rate of force | Motor pattern, connective tissue |

The climbing-specific version of GtG already exists and is the study underpinning this plan. **Abrahangs** — no-hangs with feet on the ground at light intensity, 10s on / 50s off, ~10 minutes, twice daily, at least 6 hours apart — comes from Keith Baar's collagen research: loaded connective tissue stops responding after about 10 minutes and takes ~6 hours to become responsive again. That's why the session is short and the spacing is fixed. Gilmore et al. (2024) found Abrahangs matched Max Hangs for grip strength gains, and that **doing both was additive**.

### The allocation rule
> **GtG the things you're not maxing. Max the things you're not GtG-ing.**

Fingers are the one deliberate exception — the research supports combining — but that exception has conditions (below).

### What GtG will and won't do — honest accounting

Three goals commonly attached to GtG, graded:

| Goal | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Reduce injury risk** | **Supported.** More total exposure to a movement pattern at intensities that never approach tissue tolerance builds capacity and motor quality. Also the prehab work actually gets done, which is most of the benefit. |
| **Strengthen tendons** | **Mostly false for GtG — this is your isometrics' job.** Tendon stiffness and modulus respond to *magnitude* (~90% MVC, ~3s, high localised strain); low-strain protocols are largely ineffective for this. GtG's tendon claim rests on the narrower Baar collagen-synthesis mechanism, well-evidenced in fingers, extrapolated elsewhere. See §4B. |
| **Reduce muscle fatigue** | **Half true.** GtG does not *reduce* fatigue — it is engineered to not *add* any. What it genuinely does is move volume out of sessions, so climbing days start fresher. Net it is still added stress, just very small. Expecting it to make you less tired is how people keep adding sets until it stops being free. |

### What to GtG (the committed list)

Fingers are excluded — they stay on their own protocol (§4). Abrahangs are retained only as a warm-up before Day 1 and climbing days, not as a daily habit.

| Movement | Dose | Trigger | Risk class |
|---|---|---|---|
| Push-ups | 8–12 (about half your max) | Whenever you walk past a clear floor | **Free** — loads tissue your training barely touches |
| Bodyweight or 35lb goblet squats | 10–15 | Morning, evening | **Free** — same |
| Wrist extensor work / reverse curls | 10–15 reps | Every time you make coffee | **Free** — directly protects the elbow from finger-flexor load |
| External rotations (band or light KB) | 10–12/side | Morning and evening | **Free** |
| Overcoming isometric wall press | 5s x 1–2 | Any doorway | **Free** |
| Scapular pull-ups / dead hangs on jugs | 5–8 | Walking under the bar | **Watch** — see below |
| Full pull-ups | 3–5 (well under half your max) | Walking under the bar, max 3–4x/day | **Watch** — see below |

Never to failure. If you're breathing hard or feeling a pump, the set was too long.

### The pull-up caveat

Push-ups and squats are free because nothing else in your week loads that tissue. **Pull-ups are not in that class.** They load the same elbows, shoulders, and finger flexors that your two climbing days, Day 3's isometrics, and every hangboard session already load. Daily pull-up volume stacked on top of a climber's existing pulling load is a well-worn path into medial elbow tendinopathy.

If you want a GtG pulling stimulus, prefer **scapular pull-ups and easy dead hangs** — they train position and shoulder health without adding meaningful flexor load. If you do full pull-ups, keep them genuinely trivial (3–5 reps, a handful of times a day) and drop them first at any sign of elbow soreness.

### What NOT to GtG
- Anything from Day 1's max protocol (PIMA at 100%, weighted max hangs)
- Weighted lock-offs
- Limit bouldering movement
- Fingers generally — they're on their own protocol (§4). Abrahangs stay a warm-up, not a habit.

Adding frequency to your max work is how a plateau becomes a pulley injury.

### When to drop it entirely
GtG's whole claim is that it's free. The moment it isn't, it has no argument left. Drop items in this order at the first sign of trouble:

1. **Full pull-ups** — first out, always. Any medial or lateral elbow soreness, they go.
2. **Scapular pull-ups / dead hangs** — next, if elbow or shoulder symptoms persist.
3. Everything else can usually stay; push-ups, squats, and wrist extensor work are rarely the culprit.

Other stop signals:
- Forearm or finger stiffness that doesn't clear with a normal warm-up
- Day 1 max hang numbers trending down for two consecutive sessions

Honest framing: the prehab and antagonist items in the table above are the highest-value, lowest-cost change in this whole document, and they're the part I'd actually commit to. The pulling items are optional and carry real cost. If your Day 1 numbers are climbing and your elbows are quiet, you don't need them.

---

## 9. Expected Timeline

Tendon and neural adaptations from a protocol like this typically show measurable strength gains in **4–8 weeks**, with peak-force gains in the literature ranging up to ~20–28% over 8 weeks for well-executed max-hang-style protocols. Translating that into sending V6 consistently usually takes a bit longer, since it also depends on the technique gains from your climbing days — expect to see it click somewhere in weeks 6–10 if you stay consistent.

## 10. Addenda

Sections 1–9 are the original plan. Everything below was added afterwards to fill in detail the plan referred to but never wrote down. Each addendum says where its numbers came from, and where one contradicts an earlier section it says so explicitly — the earlier text is left standing rather than quietly edited, so the disagreement stays visible.

### A. Abrahang grip rotation

§8 prescribes Abrahangs as "10s on / 50s off, ~10 minutes" and cites Gilmore et al. (2024) as the evidence. Those are two different routines. The 10s/50s cadence is Emil Abrahamsson's original 30-day experiment; the routine Gilmore et al. actually measured is the Crimpd app's version, which runs **10s on / 20s off** and reaches the same ten minutes with twice as many hangs across six grip positions. §8's own reasoning — the ~10 minute collagen-response window and the ≥6h spacing — is Baar's and applies to both.

**This addendum adopts the Crimpd/Gilmore version, and supersedes §8's 10s/50s figure.** The rest of §8's Abrahang guidance is unchanged: light intensity, feet down, twice daily at least six hours apart, a warm-up rather than a daily habit.

- Edge: **18–22mm**, the same one throughout
- Hang: **10 seconds**, rest **20 seconds**
- Intensity: feet stay on the floor at all times; load until you feel only a small strain in the forearms — roughly **40% of max**, nowhere near a limit effort
- Total: **20 hangs, 10:00**

| # | Grip | Hangs |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4-finger open | 6 |
| 2 | Front-3 open (digits 2–4) | 6 |
| 3 | Front-2 open (digits 2–3) | 2 |
| 4 | Middle-2 open (digits 3–4) | 2 |
| 5 | Front-2 half-crimp | 2 |
| 6 | Middle-2 half-crimp | 2 |

Run the grips in that order. The two-finger positions come last and are the ones to drop first: they are the highest strain per unit of load in the whole routine, and this is a warm-up.

### B. Grip in the finger warm-up

§4A ends the warm-up with "2–3 sub-maximal hangs on your training edge" and names no grip. Take those hangs in **the grips that session trains** — half-crimp and open-hand, per §4B and §4C. Warming up in a grip you are not about to load, and not warming up one you are, is the gap this closes. Nothing else about §4A changes: it is still 10–15 minutes, still jugs first, still no countdown on any stage.

### C. Scapular pull-ups and dead hangs

§8's committed list names "Scapular pull-ups / dead hangs on jugs" and gives its dose (**5–8**), its trigger (walking under the bar) and its risk class (**watch**) — and then names it as the pulling stimulus to *prefer* over full pull-ups. No section says how to perform it: §5 covers the pull day and does not include it, so it is the one movement on §8's list that is prescribed here and described nowhere.

**This addendum writes the execution down and adds no prescription.** The dose, the trigger, the risk class, and the drop order (full pull-ups first, these second, at any elbow or shoulder symptom) are §8's, unchanged. It supersedes nothing.

- Hang from a bar — or from jugs — with straight arms and shoulders relaxed into a passive hang, ears near the biceps.
- Without bending the elbows, pull the shoulder blades down and back. The body rises an inch or two; the arms do not work.
- Hold the top for a moment, then let the shoulders return to the passive hang under control.
- **5–8 reps.** Never near failure — §8's rule for the whole list applies here too.

A plain relaxed dead hang on jugs is the same item's easier form, and is what "dead hangs on jugs" means where §8 names it. §8 gives no duration for it and none is invented here: hang for as long as it stays easy, and come off well before the grip is the reason you did.
